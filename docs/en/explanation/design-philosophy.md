# Our Design Philosophy: Living Worlds, Open to Their Creators

> **Ultimate Odycer continues a tradition established by games such as *Ultima Online*, *Dwarf Fortress*, and other system-driven simulations in which the world is more than a static backdrop.**
>
> These games demonstrated that memorable experiences do not always need to be entirely scripted. They can emerge from interacting systems: ecosystems, economies, societies, factions, individual needs, environmental conditions, and the long-term consequences of player actions. A relatively simple event can propagate through the simulation and gradually become a unique story.
>
> Our goal is to carry this philosophy further. Ultimate Odycer is being designed not only as a living-world platform, but also as a highly customizable foundation for independent developers, small teams, researchers, modders, self-hosted communities, and technically curious creators.
>
> We want creators to be able to modify more than maps, characters, and visual assets. They should be able to reshape the underlying rules of the world: its social structures, artificial intelligence, progression systems, combat, crafting, economy, ecology, physics, quests, networking, and methods of interaction.
>
> This approach also extends to infrastructure. A creator should not necessarily require a large studio or an expensive cloud platform to experiment with a persistent online world. Depending on the scale of the project, an Ultimate Odycer experience should be able to run on accessible hardware, including a personal workstation, a home server, or a small self-hosted cluster, before growing toward larger deployments when necessary.

## Open by Design, Sustainable by Necessity

> We make every reasonable effort to reduce costs for creators and to release as much of the project as possible under free and open-source licenses, including permissive licenses such as MIT where appropriate.
>
> Our objective is to give independent creators genuine ownership of their work, reduce unnecessary technical and commercial barriers, and avoid locking projects into a single provider, platform, or proprietary ecosystem.
>
> When paid services, licenses, subscriptions, or usage tiers are introduced, their purpose is not to place artificial restrictions on creators. They exist to help maintain engineering quality, documentation, security, testing, support, research and development, and the acquisition of hardware required to evaluate new architectures and deployment methods under real conditions.
>
> This includes pricing associated with server capacity and concurrent-user limits, commonly referred to as CCU. We aim to keep these prices accessible, particularly for independent developers and small communities. In many cases, they remain significantly below the full economic cost of the engineering time, infrastructure, experimentation, equipment, and long-term development already invested in the project.
>
> These contributions therefore help sustain the project rather than fully compensate for everything behind it. Whenever possible, revenue is reinvested into improving the technology, validating new solutions, maintaining compatibility, strengthening reliability, and making more capabilities available to the wider community.
>
> We believe openness and sustainability should reinforce one another. Open-source foundations allow creators to understand, adapt, preserve, and extend their worlds. A measured commercial layer allows the project to continue evolving without abandoning the independence and accessibility that define its original purpose.

## A Foundation for Many Worlds

> Ultimate Odycer is not intended to define one immutable universe. It is intended to provide the foundations from which many different universes can emerge, worlds shaped by their creators, transformed by their players, and animated by systems capable of producing events that were never explicitly written in advance.
>
> Our ambition is not simply to provide another game engine or online-server framework. It is to make persistent, evolving, system-driven worlds more accessible to people who previously lacked the budget, infrastructure, team size, or proprietary technology required to build them.
